⛔️ means not recommended for that cut, for safety or texture.

## Holding and resting

- **Red meat** (beef, pork, lamb, whole or ground): hold the target for 3–5 minutes.
- **Poultry** needs longer at the same temperature: about 5 minutes at 65°C, 9–13 minutes at 63°C. From 68°C it's about a minute.
- **Thin pieces** (patties, cutlets, strips) cool too fast for the rest to count. The last minutes in the pan do count, so pull thin chicken at 68°C (154°F), turkey at 70°C (158°F), and ground red meat patties at 68°C (154°F).
- **Rest** most meats for about 5% of their cooking time: steaks for 5 minutes, roasts for 10–20 minutes. For large cuts, the rest is part of the hold.
- These temperatures are lower than USDA and CFIA consumer recommendations, which assume no hold. For anyone pregnant, elderly, very young, or immunocompromised, use 74°C (165°F) for poultry and 71°C (160°F) for ground meat.

## Notes

- **Well** is the ceiling for lean cuts: steaks, breasts, and loin roasts get tough above it.
- **Fall-apart tender** is where collagen in dark meat and connective cuts melts into gelatin. It takes time as well as temperature: a large shoulder or chuck roast can sit at 90°C for an hour or more before a fork slides through without resistance.
- Some cuts suit high temperatures and long cooks that dissolve connective tissue (chuck roast, pork shoulder or butt). Others are better rarer (bottom round roasts, loins, most steaks).
- Most fish has little connective tissue and is best rare to medium. Fish and seafood need no significant rest.
- Tuna served rare or raw is a parasite question, not a bacteria one. Buy tuna sold as previously frozen for raw use; "sushi grade" is a marketing term with no legal definition.
- Poultry can have red streaks in the dark meat near the bone even when fully cooked. It's haemoglobin leached from the marrow through weak, broken, or cut bones, and it's common in bone-in poultry that has been frozen. It's safe and has no strong flavour, even if it looks unpleasant.
