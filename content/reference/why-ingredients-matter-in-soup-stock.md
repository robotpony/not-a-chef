A lot of the principles in cookery are obvious in hindsight. Like: what can you put in stock? Assuming you want your stock to taste good, you put in things that taste good and are safe to eat. It’s important to remember that stock is really just a savoury tea, and the process itself needs to be matched with ingredients that extract well.

While the premise is simple there is a lot of well meaning, but ultimately bad advice about what goes into a good stock. For example, you see people indiscriminately freezing all of their vegetable scraps for stock without thinking about how well they extract, what they taste like, and what condition they’re in. The result can be inconsistent and disappointing.

## Ingredients to avoid

Of the various ingredients, there are some you should generally or always avoid when making stock:

1. Cruciferous vegetables (brassicas like broccoli, cabbage, kale) will tend to impart bitter flavours due to the [sulphur-like](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenylthiocarbamide) compounds in them.
2. Mouldy or rotten vegetables will tend to impart bitter flavours and may contain toxins that do not break down even with long simmers.
3. Starchy vegetables will tend to produce cloudy stock and may not affect the flavour much.
4. Many vegetable peels can be bitter and may contain pesticide used in commercial farming. Be sure to wash or soak vegetables before peeling, and taste peels before adding to a stock.
5. Many non-cruciferous leafy greens and herbs can impart bitter or off flavours given longer simmer times.
6. Certain animal parts can add strong and off flavours to stocks.

There are exceptions to these rules. Generally, and with care and balance, some chefs get great flavour out of roasted cabbage (for example). A small amount of carrot tops can add a herbaceous note to short-simmered stocks.

## Ingredients to use sparingly

Another class of stock ingredients are those that should be used sparingly. These ingredients are not unsafe or even unsavoury, but can dominate a stock either due to over-extraction or unbalanced flavours. These ingredients tend to include things like sweet vegetables (like carrots), opinionated vegetables (like fennel root or celery), or strong spices (like fennel seed, celery seed), or sweet.

## Ingredients that should work, but for whatever reason do not

There are other ingredients that seem to make sense for stock, but based on the cooking method do not tend to work well with long simmers, or under pressure. These include things like miso paste (long simmers flatten the subtle notes), kombu (which tastes better under the boil), and acids tend to do poorly.
