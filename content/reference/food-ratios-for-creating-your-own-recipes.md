Food ratios for creating your own recipes

Most recipes can be boiled down to a ratio of their ingredients. There is an interesting range for many ingredients, and in the tables below a tilde (~) denotes a moderate difference, and explicit ranges are given for ingredients that matter more. You’ll find that the ranges can lean a recipe towards one set of flavours or another, or it’s used to adjust differences between ingredients like acids that can be stronger or weaker (or sweater, saltier, etc.).

See the [food ratios worksheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1WioIgoLJITdosYp3fU7KlhytJqeXLdIpIVPg8ClIGTM/edit?gid=1047817223#gid=1047817223) to calculate ingredients based on different base amounts, which I find handy when doubling or halving recipes. I find that a digital scale is helpful, and using the metric system you can often treat grams and millilitres as equivalent for things that contain mostly water. Dry ingredient weights are not usually interchangeable with millilitres, especially dried spices.

- A wet brine is typically 5-8% salt to water, with up to 50% of that as sugar
- Wet brines can be used on both meats and vegetables (and tubers)

    - Tubers like potatoes become softer
    - Cruciferous vegetables like cauliflower taste more like themselves, and can have a softer internal texture

- Brine time is proportional to the size of the pieces of food, and brining longer both makes foods (see brine time recommendations in the [worksheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1WioIgoLJITdosYp3fU7KlhytJqeXLdIpIVPg8ClIGTM/edit?gid=1047817223#gid=1047817223))

    - more salty and sweeter, if using sugar
    - more seasoned (if using seasoning), though the effect of this depends on the solubility of the spice
    - juicier
    - softer texture
    - with a slightly watered down flavour

- For meats, longer brines make foods behave more like ham or deli meats

    - If aggressively mixed at cooler temperatures, minced meats can become more like vienna sausage (or mortadella)

- With a higher pH (more alkaline), you can achieve a bouncy asian meatball texture (with the addition of < 1% baking soda)

    - Works well for chicken or turkey patties (similar to fast food)
    - Lower pH can also be used to velvet non-minced meats

- With a lower pH (more acidic), you can achieve a softer texture if used carefully

    - Works well for southern style fried chicken (using buttermilk instead of water)

- Generally, you should use about 1% salt by weight for the food you are dry brining

    - e.g., 1kg of chicken would need 10g of dry brine spice rub
    - 10g of kosher salt is about a tablespoon (depending on the brand)

- Dry brines can tend towards salty, sweet, or balanced

    - Savoury red meats do better with less sweet (generally)
    - BBQ style foods can handle more sweet
    - Poultry and pork do well with a balanced sweet/salty

- General wet brine rules work well for dry brines (in terms of times and effect)

    - Less water is absorbed (so less flavour dilution)
    - I suspect salt/sugar absorption is slower
    - It’s simpler and less messy for weeknight cooking, and flavour is generally more meaty

- Note that dry brines cause meats to

    - expel moisture, and then
    - re-absorb it
    - if the meat is still wet, let it brine for longer
    - frozen meats will expel more moisture due to cellular tearing via freeze/thaw
