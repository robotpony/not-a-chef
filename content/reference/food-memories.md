## The Dutch Deli

I grew up in a small town of immigrants from various parts of central, eastern, and northern Europe. Our neighbours were mostly second and third generation Mennonites, with a few Dutch families sprinkled here and there. We were the outsiders, which was honestly a great way to grow up.

If I wanted to be extra honest, I would say that we were white trash. We had an old pickup sitting on blocks in the front yard. Our driveway was gravel—well, mostly weeds and gravel, always in some state of disrepair. The driveway led back to the back yard, which had a shed and two lean-tos, each filled with firewood and spiders. The shed itself had a lonely extension cord strung to it from the house, powering two old chest freezers. Beyond the shed was a small garden, and a chicken coop with a sagging roof. Beyond the coop was an empty field that occasionally housed goats or sheep, all backing on a dyke separating us from a canal and swampy land.

The term white trash didn’t quite fit who we were. My mom was Metis, or mixed-blood. A muggle. My dad was Scottish, separated by several generations from the homeland and identity. When people caught a hint that we were even partly Metis, there were jokes and sideways glances, so we avoided talking about it. We identified as Canadian, or rural Canadian, but always felt like outsiders.

Our small town had a single corner store, gas station, credit union, and deli. The deli was fantastic. We didn’t visit it often, and I imagine that it was too expensive for our modest lifestyle. But when we did visit it, the food was sublime. I remember pillowy soft kaisers, fragrant swiss cheese, and tender and savoury meats. Mustard, onions, pickles, and fermented cabbage. I loved it! I’ve always appreciated the value of a good deli, which these days are difficult to find in our area.

## The smells of India

As that small town evolved, more immigrant families moved in.

  
I remember one family bought a property owned by my best friend’s family. It was something like 15 acres of fallow land, with a large house that was built at the pinnacle of their grandparent’s careers. The house was grand, with something like 10 bedrooms, two kitchens, a library, conservatory, and countless other amenities that I found fascinating as a kid who lived in a tiny 2 bedroom turn of the century home.

It wasn’t just one family moving into that house, five families from southern India moved in. It caused a stir in the community as you can imagine, but I’ve always thought it was one of the most practical things that a large family could do. The family went on to completely change how the region was farmed, but more important to me, they changed my perspective on food.

The food, oh the food! I remember the first time I smelled the food from their kitchen. It was a confusing and intoxicating symphony of sweet and savoury. They turned milk into a sort of cheese, and ate these large beans I had never seen, all with this fresh flat bread that had burnt spots all over it. They had a strange oven on the back deck used to bake the bread, and the meat that they ate sparingly..

I didn’t know it at the time, but I think I realised that the food served in our house wasn’t in the same league as the food of southern India. Our country stews were flat and pale in comparison, our soups were flavourless, and our canned foods were bland. It wasn’t until I was older that I learned about the spices and layers that make up various Indian dishes, but the seed was planted in my head at a young age.

## Camp cooking

I remember learning to make foil camp packets at a kids camp. The recipe was simple: a handful of cut up veg, a potato cut into cubes, ¼ pound of beef, half a bouillon cube, and a paté of butter in a foil pouch (with some ground pepper). We cooked it over a fire until it smelled lovely.

That first pouch I made was so flavourful. The beef and potatoes browned and were crunchy, and the veg tasted like the rest of the pack. I don’t think I realised it at the time, but I had a whiff of what simple pleasure food could be.

I don’t think my childhood kitchen ever had bouillon, and ground beef was something that was boiled for hours so that it was safe to eat, and I don’t remember making camp pouches until I had my own kids.

We went camping at least a few times when I was younger. In the early days, we had an old, musty canvas tent that we spent as little time as possible in. Later, we bought a tent trailer that was also musty, but we would open it up in our backyard every year to air it out, so it was much nicer to spend time in.

Oddly, I don’t remember much about what we ate when we were camping. I do remember that my dad had a hibachi and a butane camp stove. I’m pretty sure the hibachi was used mostly to make hotdogs, which I likely ate plain on a white fluffy bun. This is how we ate them in grade school, and I had no idea that hotdogs had condiments until I was an adult.

I suspect that the camp stove was used to make pancakes and percolated coffee, though I mostly remember eating those tiny boxes of cereal, which opened up into an awkward bowl. I would always fight for my favourite cereals, which were corn flakes and rice crispies. I never understood why people ate fruit loops or cheerios, neither of which tasted like much.

Camping in my childhood was mostly about what we did and not what we ate; time at the lake, playing with cousins, and generally avoiding the adults. There was food, but it was simple, and mostly perfunctory and frugal.

As an adult, camping evolved into something more equally about the food and the fun, and we have a long list of camp favourites that we make every year we get up to the family lake property.  The foods we made evolved over the years, but my best memory is in developing menus that would make the kids and Ann happy, with some nostalgia, and something new. I also have fond memories of buying groceries for camping, where we always bought more than we needed.

## Garbage plates

My dad only knew how to cook a few dishes, but he was proud of each one. He loved making boxed mac and cheese. It was usually the blue generic box, and not brand named KD (Kraft Dinner for anyone not from Canada).

Dad would start by heating a few tablespoons of margarine in a tiny, janky aluminium pot, stirring in the cheese powder, adding in some fresh milk. He cooked the noodles in a larger copper-bottom pot, draining it, and then adding back his special sauce.

He served the macaroni with canned brown beans, which he spiced up with mustard and pepper, along with sliced wieners. He served the beans on the side, which I would immediately mix in with the mac as soon as the bowl hit the table. I would add generic branded catsup and preground black pepper, and would devour the bowl in a few minutes.

I still make macaroni and cheese, with baked beans and sausage. My kids call it a garbage plate, and it has always been one of their favourite comfort meals. I think of my father every time I make it, and even now it brings equal parts joy and sadness. He tried so hard.

## Slick spaghetti with a hint of oregano

I remember an old cast aluminium pot, handed down from my grandparents. It was pockmarked, with a patina of polymerized oils in the depressions. It once served as a pressure cooker that exploded in my grandmother’s kitchen, so it no longer had a lid. The handle was loose and the bolt seized, making it a death trap when loaded with a hot sauce. This pot was mostly used for my mom’s frugal spaghetti sauce, which was a simple combination of beef, puréd tomatoes, and just a hint of oregano, salt, and pepper.

And when I say a hint of oregano, it was an almost homoeopathic amount of seasoning. I remember helping my mom move in the 1990s and packing up that oregano, labelled with a best before date from somewhere in the early 1980s. I opened the bottle and smelled it, and somehow it still smelled like my childhood.

The method my mom used for making her tomato sauce was to drop a 1 pound package of mince, which I imagine was a 70/30 mix of beef and fat. We bought or traded for a quarter of beef every year, and stored the paper wrapped in stamped packages in one of the two large freezers in our shed in the backyard. I don’t remember her adding fresh onions or garlic, but I’m pretty sure a teaspoon of garlic and onion powder made their way into the sauce near the end.

That pound of beef would cook down for half an hour or so, not to brown it, but so that it was safe to eat. A lot of the lore of the kitchen in the 70s and 80s was learned in the 60s, when over cooking meat was an important step in food safety. The result was a very softened, but somehow unbrowned mass of meat and oil.

A small amount of salt and pepper were added once the beef was cooked, along with a tall pint can of store brand tomato sauce. On a good day, we used Hunt’s, usually after a case lot sale.

We knew that the sauce was done when there was a thick slick of oil on top. By today’s standards this would be unappealing, and today I usually both use a lower fat beef mixture and emulsify the oil into the beef mixture. But back then, the fat was nutrition and normal, and the calories kept us fed and warm.

The sauce was then poured over overcooked house brand spaghetti, so that water pooled around it. We stirred it up and it was delicious for a hungry young boy, and it was cheap enough that I always had seconds. Occasionally we had shelf-stable parm to dust on top, which was heaven.

I remember eating spaghetti at my aunt's house. She used green pepper, fresh onion, and (I assume) fresh garlic. I wasn’t used to the bitter and herbaceous flavour of the green pepper, but thinking back now I suspect it tasted better. I also remember having spaghetti at a friend’s house. They used more oregano, the fancy flaked style. Unfortunately the only thing I remember about it is that it was reheated in a space-aged microwave that had a big dial timer. It must have been good, though, as we always finished it quickly.
