Stock is a savoury tea. You extract flavour and gelatin from bones and vegetables into water, then concentrate it. Every stock on this site follows the same five steps: roast, extract, lift out the solids, reduce, strain. The roast sets the colour and depth, the reduction sets the strength, and the only step that really changes from one stock to the next is how long the extraction takes. Bones set that: the bigger and denser they are, the longer it takes to pull out their gelatin. No bones, no wait.

```formula
oven          | Roast    | deep brown
→ pot clock   | Extract  | bones set the time
→ bowl        | Lift out | the solids
→ pot flame   | Reduce   | by half
→ strainer    | Strain
```

## The steps

1. **Prep.** Wash the vegetables before peeling, and taste any peels before they go in. Cut roughly for a quick roast, or slice thin (a mandoline helps with the dense ones) for a darker, sweeter roast. The usual base is onion, carrot, celery, shallot, and garlic, with black peppercorns, bay, and thyme or rosemary.
2. **Roast** at 200–230°C (400–450°F), tossed with a couple of tablespoons of oil, until deep brown but not burnt, 30–45 minutes. Flip halfway. Tomato paste browned on the bones or vegetables adds colour and savouriness. *Why:* browning is where most of the flavour and colour comes from. The darker the roast, the darker the stock. Skip it for a pale stock. Scrape any fond from the trays into the pot.
3. **Extract.** Cover with water (about 4–5 L in a 6 L pressure cooker), add the peppercorns, bay, and herbs, and bring to a gentle simmer. Cook for the time in the table below, then let the pressure release naturally. *Why:* a gentle, slow extraction keeps the stock clear. A rolling boil or a fast pressure release emulsifies the fat into a cloudier, richer broth, which is only what you want for something like tonkotsu.
4. **Lift out the solids at the cooktop.** Use a spider and tongs to move the bones and vegetables into a bowl, then pour the stock through a fine-mesh strainer into a smaller pot. *Why:* straining over the sink is how a stock ends up down the drain. Every cook does it once.
5. **Steep, if using kombu or bonito.** With the heat off, steep them in the strained stock for 15–25 minutes, then lift them out. *Why:* kombu tastes better kept below a simmer.
6. **Taste.** Put a little in a small bowl with a pinch of salt or a splash of soy, and judge it against what you plan to make. The stock itself stays unsalted, so it can be reduced and used anywhere.
7. **Reduce** at a gentle simmer until it's down by half, 2–3 hours. Use the widest pot that holds it safely, check every 30 minutes or so, and skim any froth. A bone stock reduced by half sets solid and gelatinous when cold. Keep the fat for starting soups, or lift it off once it's set.
8. **Cool and store.** Cool it fast: set the pot in a sink of cold water with ice, or put an ice-filled pot or cooling paddle in the stock (see [[Food safety: time and temperature]]). Refrigerate for up to 5 days, or freeze for 3 months (up to 6).

## Extraction time by base

The steps don't change. The time does, and it follows the size of the bones. On the stovetop, allow 3–4 times the pressure cooker time.

| Base | Pressure cooker | Stovetop | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vegetable and mushroom (no bones) | 20 min, low pressure | ~1 hr | Flavour comes almost entirely from the roast; ~2:3 veg to water |
| Chicken or turkey carcasses | 30–45 min, high pressure | 2–3 hr | Backs, necks, and wing tips; the small bones give up gelatin fast |
| Beef bones | 45–100 min, high pressure | 3–6 hr | Knuckle, neck, or side cut; the bigger and denser the bones, the longer |

## What goes in, and what doesn't

Put in things that taste good and extract well. [[Why ingredients matter in stock]] goes into the details; in short:

- **Leave out** brassicas (broccoli, cabbage, kale), which turn bitter; anything mouldy or rotten; starchy vegetables, which cloud the stock without adding much; leafy greens and tender herbs, which go off over a long cook; and the pope's nose and organ meat, which add bitter or gamey notes.
- **Use sparingly** carrots (sweet), fennel and celery (they take over), and strong seeds. Garlic and celery come through stronger under pressure.
- **Add late, or not at all:** kombu and bonito go in off the heat (step 5). Acids belong in the finished soup, not the stock.

## How it fails

- **Weak:** too much water, or not enough roasting. Reduce it further.
- **Bitter:** brassicas, untasted peels, the pope's nose, or a roast taken past dark brown into burnt.
- **Cloudy:** a hard boil, a fast pressure release, or starchy vegetables. Fine for a soup base; a problem only if you want it clear.
- **Gone:** poured down the drain. Strain at the cooktop (step 4).

## Variations

**Pale stock.** Skip the roast and put the bones and vegetables straight into the water. Lighter in colour and flavour, for delicate soups and sauces.

**Ramen stock.** Chicken carcasses with ginger and a little pork fat or bones, finished with a kombu and bonito steep (step 5). Reduce at a rolling boil if you want a richer, cloudier broth.

**Other cuisines.** Ginger, star anise, and cinnamon turn the vegetable or beef base toward phở; miso and kombu toward Japanese.
