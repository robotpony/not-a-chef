This is the choice that names the poke. Salt is the oldest seasoning, and it's all the fish strictly needs. The Japanese and Chinese plantation workers who arrived from the mid-1800s brought soy sauce and sesame oil, and shoyu and sesame oil is the most common poke today ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poke_(dish))). Spicy mayo came later.

## The variables

- **Salt.** Hawaiian sea salt (pa'akai, or the red ʻalaea salt) or any coarse sea salt. Salt draws out a little moisture and firms the surface of the fish.
- **Shoyu to sesame oil.** About 2 parts soy sauce to 1 part toasted sesame oil, by volume. A little rice vinegar and grated ginger brighten it.
- **Spicy mayo.** About 3 parts Kewpie mayonnaise to 1 part sriracha, with a few drops of lime juice or rice vinegar to cut the fat.
- **Time.** Toss it just before serving, or up to an hour ahead in the fridge. Soy and salt start to cure the fish; much longer and it firms up and darkens.
- **Heat.** Chopped fresh chili, chili flakes, or wasabi, to taste.

## Options

| Option | Pick it when | Changes |
|---|---|---|
| Salt, limu, and inamona | You want the old, ocean-forward style | The lightest seasoning; the fish carries it |
| Shoyu and sesame oil (2:1) | Most of the time | Savoury and nutty; the fish counter standard |
| Spicy mayo (3:1) | You want it rich and hot | Creamy; coats the fish rather than seasoning it |
| Wasabi shoyu | You want it sharp | Wasabi stirred into the shoyu |
| Garlic shoyu | You want it bolder | Garlic oil in place of some sesame oil, with fresh chili |
