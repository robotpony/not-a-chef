A casserole is a one-pan baked dish: a starch, a filling of protein or vegetables, and a sauce that binds them. The word is French for the pan before it was the food, a diminutive of *casse*, from the Provençal for "saucepan," and it took on its current meaning around the 1870s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casserole)). Every casserole makes the same four choices: the starch, the filling, the sauce, and what goes on top before it bakes.

Most of what goes in is already cooked. That's the point. A casserole turns leftovers, or a batch base from the freezer, into a new dinner, and the bake is mostly about heating it through and browning the top. Done well, it's better than the dishes it was made from: the sauce soaks into the starch, and the top crisps.

## Why casseroles

- **They stretch scarce food.** A little meat, egg, or cheese goes a long way when it's bulked out with cheap starch (pasta, rice, potatoes) and vegetables. Casseroles earned their place in hard times, when that was the only way to feed a family.
- **They became convenience food.** In the United States the one-dish meal took off in the 20th century, especially the 1950s, alongside new lightweight metal and glass bakeware ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casserole)). Campbell's had made condensed cream of mushroom soup since 1934 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cream_of_mushroom_soup)), and canned soup did the sauce, and the casserole became the standard weeknight dinner, then fell out of fashion.
- **They travel.** A casserole freezes, carries, and reheats, so it's what you bring to a potluck, a new neighbour, or a family that's grieving.
- **They're back, from scratch.** The modern casserole swaps the canned soup for a real sauce and borrows from everywhere: enchiladas, baked ziti, curry over rice.

## A short history

Baking a starch with cheese or meat is much older than the word. The *Liber de Coquina*, from around 1300, has *de lasanis*: sheets of dough, boiled, then layered with cheese and spices ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasagna)). Cassoulet, the bean and meat bake of southwestern France, is traditionally dated to 1355, though the word itself is from the 19th century ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassoulet)).

## What isn't a casserole here

Lasagne is baked in the same dish, but it's composed layer by layer, with a filling built for it, so it's a dish of its own (see [[Vegetarian lasagna]]). The casseroles here are assembled from what you have.
