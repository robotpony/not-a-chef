I grew up in a small town of immigrants mostly from various parts of central, eastern, and northern Europe. Many of our neighbours were second- and third-generation Mennonites, with a few Dutch families sprinkled here and there. We were the outsiders, which was honestly a great way to grow up.

If I wanted to be extra honest, I would say that we were white trash. We had an old pickup sitting on blocks in the front yard. Our driveway was gravel. Well, it was mostly weeds and some gravel, always in a state of disrepair. The driveway led back to the backyard, which had a shed and two lean-tos, each filled with firewood and spiders. The shed itself had a lonely extension cord strung to it from the house, powering two old chest freezers. Beyond the shed was a small garden and a chicken coop with a sagging roof. Beyond the coop was an empty field that occasionally housed goats or sheep, all backing onto a dyke separating us from a canal and swampy land.

The term white trash didn’t quite fit who we were. My mom was Métis, or mixed-blood. A mudblood. My dad was Scottish, separated by several generations from the homeland and identity. When people caught a hint that we were even partly Métis, there were jokes and sideways glances, so we avoided talking about it. We identified as Canadian, or rural Canadian, but we always felt like outsiders.

Our small town had a single corner store, a gas station, a credit union, and a Dutch deli. The deli was fantastic. We didn’t visit it often, and I imagine that it was too expensive for our modest lifestyle. But when we did visit it, the food was sublime. I remember pillowy soft kaisers, fragrant Swiss cheese, and tender and savoury meats. Mustard, onions, pickles, and fermented cabbage. I loved it! I’ve always appreciated the value of a good deli, and good delis are hard to find in our area these days.

Deli sandwiches were the pinnacle of cuisine for me at the time. Deli-sliced Swiss cheese tasted nothing like bulk block cheddar, the only cheese I knew. The sliced turkey was moist and deeply flavourful, in stark contrast to the perfunctory holiday turkeys cooked past oblivion out of the 1960s mindset of pasteurizing food. The mustard was sharp, the sprouts fresh and earthy. It was the perfect sandwich.
