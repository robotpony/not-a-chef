None of the recipes in this cookbook started out finished. Each one began as something cooked once and written down roughly, then changed a little every time it was made: less sugar, a hotter pan, a different bean. Eventually a recipe stops changing. It becomes the version the family makes without looking, and that's when it's canon.

Some recipes here haven't got there yet. They're marked **Draft**, and they're published on purpose.

## Why publish a recipe that isn't done

The usual way to write a cookbook is to hide the work. Recipes get tested until they're right, then printed as if they came out that way. That's tidy, but it teaches the wrong lesson: that a recipe is a fixed set of instructions you either follow correctly or get wrong.

A recipe is closer to source code than scripture. It has a working version, a history of changes, and a list of things that could be better. Showing one while it's still moving makes that visible. You can see what's being tried, cook the current version, and change it yourself without feeling like you're breaking something.

## What a draft means here

- **It works.** A draft is cookable. If it weren't, it wouldn't be published.
- **It will change.** Quantities, method, even the name might move before it settles.
- **It's a good place to experiment.** A recipe that's still moving is the easiest one to make your own.

When a recipe stops changing, the draft mark comes off. Nothing else announces it, because most of the cookbook should read that way.

## Change it anyway

Canon doesn't mean finished for you. Every recipe here is a starting point. Swap the beans, cut the sugar, write your changes in the margin. That's how all of these got here.
