You have probably noticed that most recipes are hot garbage. I’m not just talking about those recipes that contain more story than method, or recipes that underestimate cooking times, temperatures, and ingredients, or even how most recipes are so poorly formatted that they’re difficult to read. I’m talking more about recipes that have no clear idea about what they are or why. And failing any semblance of self-awareness, most recipes fail to teach you much about the how either.

For example, it’s okay that there are all sorts of paths to make something like carbonara. Where most recipes fail is in not knowing where they sit on the scale of zero to carbonara, and which techniques are needed for each variation. Traditional carbonara gets its creaminess from the starches in the pasta water, the aged cheese, and eggs. It’s okay to make an approximation or another version of carbonara with heavy cream or creamed cashews too, but it’s a different dish, and a recipe should be able to talk about the reasons for the approach.

When a recipe doesn’t include that little bit of context, it forces the reader to think and fill in the gaps themselves. I remember when I was learning to cook that I wasn’t even aware that there were distinct styles or variations of a type of recipe, nor the differing techniques that were needed. Low-quality recipes didn’t help me learn technique or style, and often resulted in more failed execution than was needed (things like broken sauces, undercooked/overcooked elements, and so on).

Ideally a recipe can give you a tiny bit of background on what sort of thing it is (e.g., this is a creamy, vegan carbonara-style pasta), and then help you as a cook to understand the techniques and tools required to make it. Armed with some knowledge, you can then research and practice the techniques, and understand if it’s the style of dish you had in mind.

A recipe with some context communicates so much more to a cook, and makes their life easier. Knowing a recipe starts with a dark roux or a soffritto tells me how it will go together. These hints are great building blocks for learning to understand, execute, and adapt recipes. As shortcuts, these hints make it easier to memorize recipes, which gives you more time to focus on technique (rather than reading and re-reading the instructions).

If you’re making a vegan version of carbonara, for example, you’ll need to know how to stabilize a nut cream, and how to build a savoury base that no longer comes from cured pork.

Or if you’re making a simple North American-style carbonara, you will need to know how to work with cream in a sauce, and that bacon as a substitute can be pretty salty.

Various styles of a carbonara-style dish can be fantastic, but the sequence of ingredients and techniques is both subtly and not-so-subtly different from a more authentic or simplified version. Your expectations of the results can also differ with a bit of understanding about the what and why. A better understanding of a recipe makes it easier to understand if you’ll like it, and more importantly, how you can adapt or improve it.

Many years ago I remember my frustration when I was trying to recreate a jerk marinade. A friend regularly made a recipe handed down through generations; it was the perfect balance of savoury, sweet, and spicy. Combined with a long, slow cook time, it resulted in the most fantastic chicken I’ve had. I tested dozens of recipes, dozens of times in those early years of cooking. And while the chicken I made was edible and often delicious, it fell short of the ideal I had in mind.

As I’ve learned more about cookery, I’ve realized that you can take a recipe like jerk marinade and make it thousands of different ways. A roadside jerk shack, a high-end restaurant, and a home cook in North America will likely need different styles of recipe based on equipment, skill levels, and availability of ingredients.

It’s surprising how good some of the alternative approaches to a food can be, even when they’re not traditional. I’ve tasted great jerk marinades that use the traditional Scotch bonnet peppers (which are a bit sweet, and very hot), but I’ve also tasted great marinades that used ground cayenne or other peppers instead. Using something like cayenne vastly simplifies your prep (and it’s easier to get and store), making it appealing to restaurants and home cooks too.

## The hunt is on

When I am researching a recipe I read (and watch) hundreds of recipes. I ask myself a bunch of questions when reviewing a recipe, things like:

- How authentic is it? If it isn’t authentic, why is it different? What is the style of the variation?
- Does it have any ingredients that my guests or I can’t eat? Or does it have any ingredients that I don’t have access to?
- What style of techniques does it imply? Do I know how to pull it off?
- Will it work with the equipment I have?
- Can I prep some or all of it ahead of time? Will it freeze and reheat nicely?
- How closely does the recipe match what I have in mind?

Authenticity and regional variations are a great way to think about food, and are a step towards abstracting what you know about cooking. I generally like to learn an authentic version of something and several regional variations, to understand where a food comes from. I then start adapting a recipe for my kitchen and ingredients, which leads eventually to various fusions over time.
